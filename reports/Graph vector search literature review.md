# Epzero's Novelty Survives as Measurement, Not Method

Nearly every technique Epzero uses already exists in the literature: walking on PCA codes with a re-rank, building on codes, multi-pass construction, patience, integer scoring and machine-tuned prefetching. A paper claiming a new method would therefore be unsafe, while a paper claiming new measurements and explanations is defensible. Four results look new, and all four are conditional. **On Cascade Lake GloVe, 2 prefetched lines beat 1 line by about 1.6-1.7x on one graph in one run**, and no published study varies line count across CPUs on identical code. **A build-space effect that changes sign by dataset** (+28.3% on GIST1M, -5.4% on DBpedia) sits against LeanVec's published null, but it rests on single builds. **A same-degree second pass on a finished HNSW** has no published measurement, though the most relevant SIGMOD 2026 hubness paper remains unread. **An online, per-query re-rank stop** has no precedent in what was read, though DDC remains unread. The evaluation is careful in execution but weak in design: five rounds of variant selection on the test queries, one graph setting, three legacy datasets of four, no out-of-distribution (OOD) queries, and no separation of graph effects from search-code effects. Coverage runs through 2 November 2026, and no source was read cover to cover, so every "not found" below means not found in this search, not proven absent.

They were not independently verified, except where this report says it re-derived them.

## Building on codes is established, but a same-walk gain is not

Walking on compact codes and re-ranking on full vectors is standard practice. Building on codes is established too. LVQ says it "enables building graph indices directly from compressed vectors" ([LVQ, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.04759)), and QuIVer and Lucene BBQ do the same ([QuIVer, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.02171v1); [Elastic, primary blog](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/better-binary-quantization-lucene-elasticsearch)). Flash applies PCA then PQ-style 4-bit codes, builds on them, searches on them, and reranks on originals, with **10.4x to 22.9x build speedups on eight datasets** on a Xeon E5-2620 v3 that uses SSE by default ([Flash, VER](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18113)). That hardware limits the headline, because distance computation exceeds 90% of HNSW construction there ([Flash, passages](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18113)). Epzero's own build gains are smaller (47 s against 223 s, 95 s against 307 s) and are not novel.

The key distinction is what happens to graph quality under a fixed walk, and the record is mostly null. LVQ searched graphs built at different bit widths with float32 "to normalize for compute differences" and found "almost no decrease in throughput" at 4 or more LVQ bits, but a sharp drop for 4-bit global quantization ([LVQ, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.04759)). **LeanVec's headline search results use graphs built with uncompressed FP16 vectors**, so its 10.2x and 3.7x on gist-960-1M at recall 0.90 are walk effects, not build effects ([LeanVec, VER](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.16335)). Its Fig. 14 crosses search {LeanVec-ID, LeanVec-OOD} with build {FP16, LeanVec-ID, LeanVec-OOD} on four datasets including gist-960-1M. It reports "no noticeable differences" and calls the accuracy preservation "a surprising fact" needing theory ([LeanVec PDF, FT+FIG](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.16335); VER). That figure rules out any "first factorial" claim, although it has no full-vector walk, no plain-quantization build level, no effect sizes, and no variation of d.

Flash reports "maintaining or improving" search, yet no Flash experiment searches code-built and exact-built graphs with one routine, so its gain cannot be assigned to the graph (VER). The same confound affects its HNSW-PCA baseline, which is best on ARGILLA and worst on LAION ([Flash, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18113)). I found no clean same-walk improvement anywhere. Opposing evidence also exists: MonaVec argues "FP32 build is necessary" from noise magnitudes ([MonaVec, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.19458)), and LVQ-4 data contradicts that as a general claim. QuIVer's statement that all prior systems treat quantization as post-hoc is contradicted by LVQ, LeanVec and Flash.

Theory explains preservation, not gain. Flash's Theorem 1 and LVQ's Proposition 1 bound harm ([Flash, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18113); [LVQ, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.04759)). Hubness skew stays flat under reduction until intrinsic dimension is reached, but those quotes come from crude text extraction ([Radovanović et al., FT](https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/radovanovic10a.html)). Nothing read predicts a gain, and no source tests denoising, metric alignment or hubness change as the cause.

Query projection cost is treated casually. LeanVec calls it negligible but measures nothing, and recommends d in [160, 256] (VER), although its own selections run from 96 on deep-256-1M to 320 on laion-512-1M, where it reports no significant gain ([LeanVec, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.16335)). ADSampling puts rotation at no more than 6.18% of HNSW cost ([ADSampling, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.09855)), but the distance-comparison-operation (DCO) benchmark finds that analysis underestimates O(D²) projection cost ([DCO benchmark, VER](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02801)). SymphonyQG sidesteps the issue with a Fast JLT ([SymphonyQG, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12229)), which does not apply to a PCA basis. Choosing d remains "a challenging open problem" ([DR survey, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.13491)), solved today by sweeps or variance rules.

Integer scoring is crowded. Glass scores int8 against int8 with a global scale, and keeps SQ8 float queries against per-dimension codes ([pyglass, SRC](https://github.com/zilliztech/pyglass)). LoRANN is the closest prior art for quantized projection: int8 query and int8 model matrices on VPDPBUSD with absmax scaling per vector, after what appears to be a float PCA ([LoRANN, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18926v1)). That reading of W_s staying float is an inference and should be confirmed in its code. RaBitQ, SAQ and BBQ cover 1-bit and multi-bit variants, none on Epzero's combination ([RaBitQ, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12497); [SAQ, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12086)).

Per-edge coding is the main design competitor. SymphonyQG keeps one code per directed edge with no explicit re-rank, reaching **1.5x-4.5x over the best baseline at 95% recall, K=10, and 3.5x-17x over HNSWlib**, at a memory figure of 140.3 GiB versus 59.2 GiB on Deep-100M ([SymphonyQG, VER](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12229)). The verified pair does not name which side is which; the per-edge design suggests the larger figure is SymphonyQG's, and that should be confirmed before quoting. PAG reports very low SymphonyQG recall at 1536 and 3072 dimensions and blames a 16-bit overflow ([PAG, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06660)). That is a code-defect claim, not evidence against 1-bit walks. Learned projections win clearly only under OOD queries ([LeanVec, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.16335); [GleanVec, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.22347)), and QPAD's comparison excludes standard PCA altogether ([QPAD, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.16335)), so PCA remains the right in-distribution baseline.

## Multi-pass construction is old, while a same-degree HNSW pass is unpublished

Vamana made two passes in 2019, the first at alpha 1 and the second at a user alpha, "motivated by our observation that the second pass improves the graph quality", though the passages read give no isolated number ([DiskANN, T](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/09853c7fb1d3f8ee67a61b6bf4a7f8e6-Paper.pdf)). ParlayANN's two-pass flag defaults to off ([neighborsTime.C, C](https://github.com/cmuparlay/ParlayANN/blob/main/algorithms/bench/neighborsTime.C)). Revisiting the Index Construction finds iterative construction gives marginal gains after two iterations, builds FastHNSW 1.6x to 4.6x faster, and argues RNG pruning is not designed for candidate acquisition ([Revisiting, VER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01231)). It also states HNSW's candidate quality is "limited to 0.5" by incremental insertion (T). That bound explains why a post-build search helps, but it concerns expected candidate recall and does not prove early nodes are specifically damaged. No per-node measurement by insertion time was found. tau-MNG permits an HNSW as its starting graph ([tau-MG, T](https://www.comp.hkbu.edu.hk/~edisonchan/publication/tau-MNG.pdf)), and SymphonyQG's construction is itself iterative ([SymphonyQG, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12229)).

The nearest precedents show the idea has occurred to others, so "new idea" is unsafe. FastHNSW rebuilds by layer rather than running a second pass. The GraphANNS-Hubness repository contains `BuildFromHnsw()`, which searches every node on the finished graph at `ef_construction * 4`, reselects at `maxM0_` and overwrites level 0, but nothing in that repository calls it ([repo, C](https://github.com/H40NaN/GraphANNS-Hubness)). hnswlib's `updatePoint` could emulate a pass ([hnswalg.h, C](https://github.com/nmslib/hnswlib/blob/master/hnswlib/hnswalg.h)), and VSAG's `build_with_cache` refines from a previous build ([hgraph.h, C](https://github.com/antgroup/vsag/blob/main/src/algorithm/hgraph/hgraph.h)). Among hnswlib, FAISS, USearch, VSAG, Glass, Knowhere and Lucene, none offers a general post-build pass that re-searches every node, though some FAISS bodies were read by name only, and pgvector, Qdrant, Weaviate and Vespa were not checked.

**The biggest novelty risk across this topic is the SIGMOD 2026 hubness paper, blocked at HTTP 403 and known only from search summaries and its README** ([ACM, S](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3802120)). It may report a search-based variant. Its published post-processing path, `Optbuild()`, does no new search.

Pruning results favor Epzero's negative finding. At maximum degree 60, RND and MOND outperform RRND, with alpha tuned from 1 to 2 ([Azizi et al., T](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05575)). alpha-CNG wins at M of 50-100 (W for the parameters) with an adaptive schedule, reporting 2.28x and 2.88x maximum speedups ([alpha-CNG, T](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05975)). HNSW defines keepPrunedConnections with no measured gain in the passages read ([HNSW, T](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09320)), and Xiao et al. argue against two-hop candidates for update repair ([Xiao et al., T](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07871)). That argument concerns replacing edges, while Epzero fills free slots.

On hubs and entry points, FlatNav reports results essentially identical to hnswlib without hierarchy on 13 datasets up to 100M, and conjectures the largest gains come from base-graph connectivity ([FlatNav, T](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01940)). That framing suits a base-layer pass. Azizi et al. find K-sampled random seeds and stacked NSW best ([Azizi et al., T](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05575)), and their later work notes hierarchy helps mainly at billion scale ([Azizi et al. 2025, T](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05750)). When to Repair is a useful cautionary case: its authors withdrew a v1 claim after matched-budget comparison, and every 95% CI over four seeds included zero ([When to Repair, T](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00728)).

**A correction to the notes matters here.** The construction notes rest the Glass verdict on no downloaded paper mentioning Glass. That is true only of those roughly 30 texts. VIBE evaluates Glass, reporting 81% recall on the hardest 100 arxiv-nomic queries and under 50% on attention MIPS sets ([VIBE, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17810)), and LoRANN's curves cross it ([LoRANN, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18926v1)). Glass has no paper of its own, but it is an established baseline, so beating it is a measurement rather than a novelty.

## Hardware dependence is widely assumed and rarely measured on identical code

Only Bang for the Buck is a true multi-CPU graph study, with six AWS CPUs running FAISS IVF and USearch HNSW single-threaded ([Bang for the Buck, passages](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07621v1)). Rankings do reverse there. Zen 4 is "the clear winner" on IVF and "performs the worst" on HNSW float32, and SQ8 in FAISS IVF helps Zen 4 and Sapphire Rapids yet drops Graviton3 from 27.4 to 9.6 QPS because the ARM path is scalar. Those reversals come from kernels and library support, and the study varies no prefetch setting. The DCO benchmark's "diverse hardware" is **one CPU model, a Ryzen 9 5950X, with SIMD levels varied**, and rankings flip with SIMD ([DCO benchmark, VER](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02801)). KBest compares different code on different CPUs ([KBest, passages](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03016v2)), and VIBE names the gap outright: "relative implementation performance may differ on other hardware" ([VIBE, passages](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17810)).

Prefetching is established at about +15-20% alone and up to 2x when tuned ([VSAG, passages](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17911v3); [SVS, passages](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04759)). In VSAG's cumulative table, tuning environment parameters adds +35% on GIST1M and +39% on SIFT1M, more than the mechanism itself. Libraries disagree on lines: hnswlib fetches the first line, DiskANN floor(size/64), SVS ceil(bytes/64) plus one for short unaligned sizes, and Glass, NGT and VSAG tune ([hnswalg.h, code](https://github.com/nmslib/hnswlib/blob/master/hnswlib/hnswalg.h); [utils.h, code](https://github.com/microsoft/DiskANN/blob/cpp_main/include/utils.h); [prefetch.h, code](https://github.com/intel/ScalableVectorSearch/blob/main/include/svs/lib/prefetch.h)). The closest number for first-line against whole-vector is SVS PR 361, 946 to 729 QPS on cohere-768 at recall 0.95, on an unstated CPU ([PR 361, code/PR](https://github.com/intel/ScalableVectorSearch/pull/361)). Flash does not mention prefetching (VER). So VSAG's statement that optimal prefetch settings vary by environment is an assertion: its ablation table is one machine, and it reports no per-CPU tuned values in the sections read.

Where fetch and compute are separated, memory dominates, and "distance dominates" claims often include fetching. VSAG attributes 63.02% of search time to memory access, while ADSampling's 77.2-87.6% DCO share includes fetches ([VSAG, passages](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17911v3); [ADSampling, passages](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.09855)). VSAG's own numbers need care, since its text ties 67.42% L3 misses to the baseline while its table gives 93.89% for the baseline and 67.42% after quantization, and it also gives 2337 and 2377 for one result.

AVX-512 downclocking on Skylake and Cascade Lake is documented, and it is largely gone from Ice Lake on ([Downs, summary](https://travisdowns.github.io/blog/2020/08/19/icl-avx512-freq.html); [Bang for the Buck, passages](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07621v1)). Masked loads are cheap in microbenchmarks, at 0.50 cycles and 2 µops on Cascade Lake ([uops.info, passages](https://uops.info/html-instr/VMOVDQU8_YMM_K_M256.html)), and no ANN work calls them a bottleneck. Those throughput numbers exclude line splits and misses, so they do not settle Epzero's case. Scaling work shows bandwidth limits and chiplet effects: SVS float16 saturates at 40 threads ([SVS, passages](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04759)), and CCD-aware dispatch reaches up to 3.7x ([CCD paper, passages](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.10090)). No x86 comparison of local, remote and interleaved placement for graph search was found, and single-thread prefetch results may not carry to full-core runs. Layout effects are conditional: VSAG's redundant storage helps GIST1M and hurts SIFT1M ([VSAG, passages](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17911v3)), and huge pages give 20% at 100M and 90% at 1B in SVS ([SVS, passages](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04759)).

### The Epzero prefetch evidence is stronger on one CPU and weaker across two

I re-derived the Cascade Lake grid from `bench/results/pace/gold6226/glove-100-angular-speed-round5.json` by log-linear interpolation, using 10,000 queries, three rounds, and one saved graph.

| Setting (depth.lines) | 0.80 | 0.85 | 0.90 | 0.93 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glass QPS | 9,610 | 5,758 | 2,908 | 1,665 |
| all.1 | 0.60x | 0.65x | 0.68x | 0.75x |
| 8.1 | 0.59x | 0.64x | 0.67x | 0.74x |
| 8.2 | 1.02x | 1.09x | 1.14x | 1.21x |
| all.2 | 0.98x | 1.06x | 1.11x | 1.19x |
| 8.3 | 0.98x | 1.05x | 1.09x | 1.17x |
| 2.2 | 0.89x | 0.96x | 1.00x | 1.08x |

Three things follow. The supplied 0.60-0.75x and 1.02-1.21x ranges match all.1 and 8.2, which differ slightly in depth, so the clean within-depth effect of a second line is **1.64-1.72x at depth 8 and 1.59-1.64x at depth all**. A third line loses ground, which is what you would expect if it fetches links the walk does not need. And **depth 2 is about 12-15% behind depth 8**, which contradicts the earlier log statement that the server's cores prefer a shallow window, the opposite of the laptop's. The earlier 13-17% and 15-17% tuner gains may have come from lines rather than depth, and the "second reversal in depth" should not be claimed.

The cross-CPU half is weaker than "best line count reverses". On the i5-13420H under WSL2, the log's evidence is that the tuner found "nothing on SQ8 codes", and the tuner keeps an alternative only at 3% or more. An older laptop entry says 64 to 512 bytes "all measured the same". So the supported statement is that **one line suffices on Raptor Lake, with no tested alternative gaining 3%, while it costs about 1.6x on Cascade Lake**. That is a sensitivity contrast, not a proven strict reversal, until the same grid runs on both machines. The WSL2 nested-paging difference remains uncontrolled, and the record is 104 bytes, so "2 lines" means whole-record. The adjacent-line hypothesis, that line 2 is the 128-byte pair of line 1, is untested (MSR 0x1A4 was seen at search level only, [Intel forum, search](https://community.intel.com/t5/Intel-ISA-Extensions/Disabling-HW-prefetcher/m-p/1176713)).

### The 31% loss has no proven cause

The 256-bit VNNI kernel ran 31% slower than AVX2 on GloVe-100 and 2.5% faster on DEEP10M. `docs/performance.md` headlines this "the masked tail, settled", and the kernel comment agrees, but that attribution is unproven. A cache-line split is a live alternative: the tail load covers record bytes 100-131 and crosses the line at 128 on every scored node, while 96-dimension records have no tail. Epzero's own log supports this direction, since block scoring that read a third line cost -34% on GloVe-100 and -38% on DEEP10M. Two gaps remain. No source says whether masked-out bytes in a split load still touch the second line, and the contrast compares two datasets (100-d 1.2M against 96-d 10M), so dimension is not the only difference. Whether Cascade Lake's weakness on short vectors comes from arithmetic is also not settled by this result.

## Adaptive gains shrink to single digits against an honestly tuned baseline

Headline early-termination speedups of 3-15x mostly compare against a search set for higher recall than the target, or use a hard query mix. The table below separates the baselines.

| Method (status) | Baseline | Same recall? | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| LAET (PARTIAL) | Fixed efSearch, swept | Yes | Up to 7.1x, recall-at-1, 95-100% |
| VSAG QLP (PARTIAL) | Smallest ef reaching target | Yes | +0.6% to +7.2% (computed) |
| DARTH vs REM (PARTIAL) | efS tuned per target and noise | Yes, hard workload | Up to 5.8x, 3.1x average |
| DARTH vs HNSW (PARTIAL) | efS for recall >= 0.99 | No | Up to about 14.6x, median 5.7x |
| PiP (FULL) | Same ef, no retuning | Approximately | +18% to +61% (computed) |
| Busolin et al. (PARTIAL) | Fixed clusters at R*@1=0.95 | Yes, on IVF | 2.95x to 5.13x |
| Terminus (PARTIAL) | IO-Budget | Yes, on RAG accuracy | 1.16x to 1.4x |
| Ada-ef (PARTIAL) | DARTH | Yes | Up to 4x lower workload latency |

Sources are [LAET](https://www.pdl.cmu.edu/ftp/BigLearning/mod0246-liA.pdf), [VSAG](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17911), [DARTH](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19001v2), [PiP](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jimmylin/publications/Teofili_Lin_ECIR2025.pdf), [Busolin et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04981v1), [Terminus](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~mfreed/docs/terminus-mlsys26.pdf) and [Ada-ef](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06636). DARTH states both 14.6x and "up to 15x". Ada-ef contradicts DARTH's reliability claim on modern embeddings, finding recall "considerably lower" on GloVe-100. Terminus adds independent evidence for small plain-recall gains, since "the Recall-I/O curves across all systems are nearly identical". The inference is that gains scale with query-hardness skew, and that 5-15% is normal for rule-based methods on default workloads. VSAG claims personalized parameters could yield 3-5x, yet realizes 0.6-7.2%, a citable gap that stays open.

Patience is production practice: Lucene ships `PatienceKnnVectorQuery` ([Lucene API](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_2_2/core/org/apache/lucene/search/PatienceKnnVectorQuery.html)), and Elasticsearch 9.3 enables an adaptive form by default ([Elastic, summariser only](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/hnsw-elasticsearch-adaptive-early-termination)).

Re-rank depth varying per query is established, and early stops exist in four forms. Rank stability appears in FusionANNS, which stops when the top-k change rate stays below epsilon ([FusionANNS, PARTIAL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.16576)). A fixed separation test appears in AQR-HNSW ([AQR-HNSW, PARTIAL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21600)). Per-candidate bounds appear in RaBitQ and MRQ ([RaBitQ, FT in the compression notes](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12497); [MRQ, PARTIAL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.06158)). Offline-fitted calibration appears in FaTRQ ([FaTRQ, PARTIAL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.09985)). Elastic's DiskBBQ measures Gaussian noise from exact-versus-quantized gaps on a sample, but per segment at merge time, and uses it to set oversampling once ([Elastic, summariser only](https://www.elastic.co/search-labs/blog/vector-quantization-auto-calibration-diskbbq)). The two topic notes disagree on RaBitQ's status, and the compression notes read its text, leaving **DDC as the one key unread method** (NOT READ, known via VSAG).

Distance-comparison methods are conditional. With SIMD, HNSW speedups fall from 1.9-3.1x to 1.1-2.1x ([DCO benchmark, PARTIAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02801v1)), SkipComputing agrees (SEARCH), and Panorama-HNSW drops to 0.80x without PCA yet gains 2.83x with it ([Panorama, PARTIAL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.00566)). Failure conditions are documented: cheap compute, small skipped fractions, very high dimension, OOD queries, and non-contiguous layout. These results scan full-precision floats. The reading that 8-bit codes shrink the savings further is mine, and no source tested it.

Black-box tuners are mature, reaching 98.03-99.97% of oracle QPS in CHAT on one machine ([CHAT, PARTIAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04630v1)). VDTuner tunes 16 Milvus parameters ([VDTuner, PARTIAL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.10413)). VSAG, SVS and Glass adapt to a CPU only by measuring on it. No tuner found transfers across CPUs without re-measuring or models hardware explicitly, and no primary evaluation of SVS calibration or Glass `optimize` was found.

## The 2026 evaluation bar is VIBE's design plus hardware and scale

VIBE v3 is the current reference design, with 22 implementations, 11 in-distribution and 8 OOD datasets, held-out queries, one hyperparameter grid across all datasets, Pareto frontiers, one thread on a NUMA-bound socket, five repetitions using the minimum query time, and Wilcoxon tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction ([VIBE, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17810)). Azizi et al. add scale to 1B and a trimmed mean over six runs ([Azizi et al., FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05575v2)). Repetition summaries are not standardized, and query counts run from 100 to 10,000.

SIFT, GIST and GloVe are now treated as legacy. VIBE says such data is "not representative of modern applications", and Iceberg and PAG agree ([VIBE, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17810); [Iceberg, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12980); [PAG, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06660)). They retain value as hardness controls, with GIST at intrinsic dimension 42.5 against OpenAI ada-002 at 46.5 and SIFT at 19.2 ([Power Law, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02143)). VIBE itself keeps a GloVe set. A 2019 counterpoint, that real datasets are not diverse, is known only from a summary ([Aumüller and Ceccarello, SS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.07387)).

OOD queries are the sharpest risk for a quantized engine. VIBE names the mechanism directly: a quantizer fitted to the corpus "can produce larger errors in the estimated candidate distances, steering graph traversal along less useful paths" ([VIBE, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17810)). ParlayANN saw graphs 12.2 to 19.6 times slower on an OOD dataset ([ParlayANN, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.04359)). No source quantifies SQ8 specifically under OOD.

Recall@k remains primary, but it is no longer sufficient. At T=0.95 and k=100, satisfying Recall costs 1.86x to 9.36x more distance computations than Ratio, with rankings "largely unchanged" and gaps not preserved, though this is a v1 preprint ([Recall What Matters, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04522)). Average recall hides fragility ([Wang et al., FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.00379); [RCheck, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25185)). Single-size evaluation is now a named weakness: at fixed recall, distance computations follow N^c on eight datasets, and the exponent varies with recall, intrinsic dimension, M and efc ([Power Law, FT](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02143)).

The classic warning, Kriegel et al.'s "Are we comparing algorithms or implementations?", was not read ([record, SS](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0219-1377/volumes-and-issues/52/paper-detail/16814)), and no specifics should be cited from it. Modern evidence is scattered across ParlayANN, Azizi et al.'s implementation section and PLASMA (SS), and no 2025-2026 paper quantifies HNSW implementation effects across libraries. PVLDB Volume 20 requires a full reproducibility package at initial submission, and EA&B papers get "no excuses" ([PVLDB guidelines, OFFICIAL](https://vldb.org/2027/submission-guidelines.html)). No published collection of reviewer objections exists, so any such list is inference.

## Threats to validity are mostly design threats, not measurement threats

The execution holds up well: `--exclusive` nodes, one search thread, `numactl` socket binding, interleaved rounds with rotating library order, raw `qps_samples`, and 10,000 queries where available. Gold 6226 runs at a clock the PACE README records as fixed at 2.7 GHz. The novelty claims carry a separate threat of their own. Each rests on keyword-located reading, not on complete papers, and on searches that did not systematically cover patents, non-English venues or closed engines. Unread at this date are the hubness paper, DDC, DARTH+, Kriegel et al., PiPNN and SmartANN beyond search summaries, and SkipComputing beyond a summary. LoRANN surfaced late, on a differently worded query, which shows that a similar miss remains plausible.

## Conclusion

The advisor's decision is clearer than the engineering history suggests. Epzero should not be presented as a faster library with novel algorithms, because reviewers can cite prior art for each component and the Glass ratios cannot yet be assigned to any one component. It can be presented as an empirical study of what actually drives in-memory graph search: which memory-system settings fail to transfer across CPUs, whether the build space changes a graph, and how much of a multiple is implementation. That framing also tolerates failure, since a clean null on build space or a host artifact in prefetching still produces usable results.

The re-derivation changed the picture in both directions, which is itself a warning. The Cascade Lake prefetch effect became sharper, while the depth reversal disappeared and the cross-CPU claim narrowed. Two findings described as settled or new in the project log, the masked tail and the Glass gap in the literature, did not hold at that strength. Any number headed for a paper should therefore be re-derived from raw results, and any "not found" should be retested against the unread papers before submission.
